## **Unprecedented Scottish megafire leads to widespread peat carbon losses** 

Johanna Schoenecker[1] *, Martin J. Baur[1] , Juliana Kohli[1] , Sarah Baker[2] , Matthew Jones[3] , Sander Veraverbeke[4] , Alexandra G. Konings[5] & Adam Pellegrini[1,5] *

- 1 **Drier and warmer climates have allowed fires to increasingly burn carbon-dense** 2 **peatland ecosystems. Here, we document the first megafire in the United Kingdom,** 3 **which spread rapidly and burned severely across peatlands in Scotland with** 

- 4 **anomalously low soil moisture, emitting 39,338 MgC (25,250–64,565 MgC). Peat** 

- 5 **combustion contributed 85% of total emissions, suggesting drier climates increase** 

- 6 **fire emissions from peat, which are functionally irrecoverable on end-of-century** 7 **timescales most relevant to climate change mitigation.** 

- 8 

- 9 Fires are becoming larger and more severe in many ecosystems worldwide[1] , resulting in 

- 10 increasing ecosystem impacts and carbon emissions[2,3] . Changing wildfire regimes in 

- 11 peatlands are especially concerning given that these ecosystems rarely burned historically; 12 however, when peatlands burn, they can produce large carbon emissions when organic soil 

- 13 is combusted[4–6] (e.g., 78% of total fire emissions in northern systems[7] ) and require 

- 14 centuries-millennia to re-sequester carbon in organic soils[8,9.] The multi-century recovery of 15 carbon stocks in peat means that emissions can be considered ‘irrecoverable’ from the 16 standpoint of climate mitigation initiatives, which typically focus on achieving a net zero 17 exchange of carbon between the land and atmosphere during the current century[1] . 

- 18 

- 19 High soil moisture in peatlands, sustained even during climatological dry seasons, protects 20 belowground carbon stocks from burning most of the time[2] . In recent decades, tropical 21 peatlands have experienced fires that combust peat, with extensive drainage for agriculture 22 and occurrence of El Niños leading to low soil moisture and severe fires[3,4] , while northern 23 peatlands have been less affected. However, warmer and drier climates in northern 

- 24 peatlands[10] are increasing the occurrence of conditions conducive to wildfire (e.g., Canada’s 25 2023 wildfire season)[11] which lead to higher emissions from peat carbon stocks[5] . Here, we 26 document the occurrence of the largest wildfire in at least the last twenty years (MODIS era) 27 on peatland in the United Kingdom in June 2025 and argue that it points towards a possible 28 shift in the UK’s fire regime and a bellwether for temperate and northern peatlands globally, 29 many of which are forecast to experience drier and hotter climates[11,12] . 

- 30 

- 31 The Dava Moor Fire, formed by the merger of two independently ignited fires at Dava and 32 Carrbridge, northern Scotland, was the largest wildfire in UK history. Between June 28 and 33 July 1, 2025, it burned approximately 10,000 hectares, making it the UK’s first megafire 34 under a common size-threshold definition[6] . The fire was roughly twice the size of the next 35 largest fire in the UK (Flow Country wildfire in 2019, recorded over the last 20 years in the 36 MODIS era) and equivalent to the historical average of the UK’s total annual burned area[7] 37 (2001-2021, _Methods_ , Extended Data 1). The wildfire spread rapidly and burned severely, 

- 38 with the full extent of the fire burning in just four days and over 79% of the total burned area 39 classified as high severity ( _Methods_ , Extended Data 2). In a country where fires are usually 40 small, often managed, and not severe[13] , the scale of this wildfire is unprecedented in the 41 UK. 

- 42 

- 43 The wildfire occurred under very dry climate conditions. Soil moisture observations by the 44 Soil Moisture Active Passive (SMAP) satellite[8,9] ( _Methods_ , Figure 1a,b) illustrate that much 45 of Scotland was experiencing unusually dry soil moisture conditions preceding the fire. At the 46 time and location of the fire, soil moisture was 1.9 standard deviations below the decadal 47 average for that month in that location (Figure 1c). The low soil moisture at the time of the 

- 48 fire is consistent with dry conditions throughout 2025 following and a relatively dry 202449 2025 winter (precipitation was also very low, _Extended Data 3_ ). Meteorological conditions— 

- 50 assessed via the Fire Weather Index (FWI)[10] —illustrated that the location where the wildfire 51 occurred had higher FWI values that other areas in Scotland, and temporally that the 

- 52 anomaly was substantially higher in May and marginally higher in June (1.3 and 0.4 standard 53 deviations above decadal average, respectively, Extended Data 4). Concurrently, estimated 54 aboveground fuel load per unit area was 1.2 standard deviations above the decadal average 55 for June (estimated via vegetation optical depth[11] ; Extended Data 5). Thus, fuel 

- 56 accumulation likely contributed to the rapid spread, but the dry climatic conditions primed the 57 system to burn. 

- 58 

- 59 Nearly 80% of the total wildfire area was classified as severely burned (determined via 

- 60 differenced Normalized Burn Ratio (dNBR) from Sentinel-2 at 20 m resolution; Figure 2a,b, 

- 61 Table S1). Overlaying the high-resolution satellite data with a 10 m land cover map from 62 2023[12] , we partitioned burn severity among ecosystem types (Figure 2b). Shrubland (land 63 cover type of moorlands and heathlands) dominated the burned area (83% of burned area, 64 Figure 2b, Table S1). Bogs accounted for 10% of burned area (Figure 2b, Table S1; bogs 65 are defined as areas where peat depths exceed 0.50 m), while forests, woodlands and other 66 vegetation types accounted for just 6% of burned area. 

- 67 

- 68 Even in typically wet bogs, 58% of the burned area was high severity (Figure 2b, Table S1). 69 Consistent with the low soil moisture (Figure 1) and the high severity (calculated based on 70 spectral changes of visible biomass), the fire burned into the peat (validated with 112 field 

- 71 measurements ca. one month after the fire; _Methods_ , Extended Data 6-7). Our field 

- 72 sampling confirmed the presence of peat in 100% of randomly sampled points, and we were 

- 73 able to measure peat burn depths in areas classed as bog, moorlands and heathlands, and 

- 74 forests and woodlands (Extended Data 8-9). 

- 75 

- 76 We next quantified carbon emissions using a bottom-up emissions calculation based on soil 77 moisture, burn depth, carbon density and combustibility of different land cover classes[12] 

- 78 ( _Methods)_ . We constrained burn depth with 112 field measurements and peat bulk density 

- 79 with 20 measurements from a nearby intact peatland ( _Methods_ ). We also compared our 

- 80 locally-optimised bottom-up estimates to those from a global top-down fire emissions product 

- 81 based on observations of fire radiative power emitted from burned areas (Global Fire 

- 82 Assimilation System; GFAS[13] ) and to a an existing UK-specific peat combustion model from 83 Baker et al. ([12] ; referred to here as the Baker Peat Combustion Model). 

- 84 

- 85 In this single fire, carbon emissions were 39,338 MgC (25,250 – 64,565 MgC based on the 

- 86 25[th] and 75[th] quantiles of measured burn depths; Figure 2c-d, Table S2). Most emissions 

- 87 arose from combustion of peat (85% of total emissions, 31,294 MgC, Figure 2d, Table S2). 

- 88 Shrublands, which contributed the most to burned area, also contributed the most to 

- 89 emissions; surprisingly, a large fraction of total emissions from shrublands arose form 

- 90 belowground peat combustion whereas the combustion of aboveground vegetation 

- 91 combustion was relatively small (Figure 2d, Table S2). Bogs, which are usually too wet to 

- 92 burn, emitted 2,463 MgC (80% coming from burning into the peat; Figure 2d, Table S2). 93 Combustion of peat was the predominant loss pathway in this fire and even occurred in 94 historically wet bogs due to the low soil moisture. 

- 95 

- 96 Compared with our locally optimised emissions estimates, the Baker Peat Combustion 

- 97 Model over-estimated burn depths (predicting burn depths of ca. 6 cm vs. our observed 

- 98 median depths of less than 2 cm even in high severity areas, Extended Data, 8,9, Table S3). 99 However, because the Baker Peat Combustion Model did not quantify belowground 

- 100 emissions from non-bog areas, the total wildfire emissions were comparable to our 

- 101 emissions using median field burn depths across bog as well as moorland and heathland 

- 102 land cover types (32,960 MgC vs. 39,338 MgC). GFAS estimated emissions based on fire 

- 103 radiative power were 30,000 MgC. Thus, the GFAS and peat model estimates underestimate 104 emissions by 15% and 24%, respectively, relative to our estimates. 

- 105 

- 106 Many lines of evidence point to the 2025 summer climate conditions being more likely in the 107 present climate than in a pre-industrial climate, and that conditions like this will be more 108 common in the future, especially under higher emissions scenarios. First, human-caused 

- 109 climate change has increased the risk of wildfire in the UK (e.g., six-fold in 2022 ref.[11] ). 

- 110 Second, the rising FWI, especially in the summer, appears to be a trend both locally (area 

- 111 around Morayshire wildfire, Extended Data 10) and regionally (Europe[14] ). Even under low 

- 112 emission scenarios, the FWI in Europe is expected to rise by 24% by 2050[14] . Finally, the UK 113 is projected to experience drier and hotter summers and more droughts (e.g., once every 20 114 years to once every three years by 2040[14] ), which are conditions that lead to lower soil 

- 115 moisture as a pre-requisite to peat burning[2] . Consequently, fires are likely to not only grow 116 larger but also burn more severely into peat. Both in the UK and globally, however, most fire 117 models and fire weather indices are not tuned to peatland fires. For example, a prior analysis 118 showed that some existing indices (e.g., the initial spread index[12] ) were correlated with re119 analysis soil moisture and modelled peat emissions, but the correlations were weak. 

- 120 

- 121 Our findings have ramifications for climate change and the policy for reducing emissions. 122 The peat being combusted is often hundreds to thousands of years old[9] , requiring equivalent 123 or longer time to be recovered. Thus, the transition towards more C emissions from peat 124 means that the net-emission impact of wildfires is rising. Along these lines, increasing 125 peatland fires under dry conditions produces carbon emissions that for the purposes of 126 climate mitigation is ‘irrecoverable’ because it takes centuries to reaccumulate—far 127 exceeding the timeline of climate goals from 2050-2100. UK climate and land use policy 128 focuses on re-wetting and restoring peatlands (e.g., investing £50M to restore 35k hectares 129 in 2023), which in theory should help mitigate the risk of peat burning during fires. However, 130 peatlands might remain vulnerable to extreme soil moisture deficit, necessitating policy 131 makers to consider spatial projections in soil moisture changes when investing in restoration 132 projects. Thus, preventing wildfires on peat should be prioritised, and factors such as 133 projections on how soil moisture might change should be considered. 

- 134 

- 135 We propose that these findings highlight the UK is experiencing extreme wildfire conditions 136 that make peatlands especially vulnerable and serves as a bellwether for how temperate and 137 northern peatlands may change with global warming. The rising occurrence of very hot and 138 dry summers has shattered the historically wet climate conditions of many temperate and 139 northern peatlands[15] . This has corresponded with extensive wildfires and associated carbon 140 emissions from these regions[16,17] . However, comparison between our field-constrained 141 estimates and existing models for quantifying emissions demonstrated existing models 142 underestimate carbon losses by roughly a quarter. Taken together, our findings demonstrate 143 that as soil moisture declines, peat losses can rapidly overtake aboveground combustion as 144 the dominant source of carbon emissions in wildfires.

## **Methods summary** 

235 

236 **Climate conditions and fuel load** 

237 To assess the climatic and environmental conditions around the Dava Moor fire we use soil 238 moisture and vegetation optical depth (VOD) from NASA’s Soil Moisture Active Passive 239 (SMAP) mission, which uses on observations in the microwave spectrum to track water in 240 soils and vegetation. SMAP soil moisture has been successfully used to predict burned area 241 in peatlands and to track fire across ecosystems[4,18] . VOD is strongly related to vegetation 242 water content and can give information on fuel load and fuel moisture[19] . Here we use SMAP 243 soil moisture from the baseline single channel algorithm (SMAP-SCA), while VOD is taken 244 from the dual channel algorithm (DCA), both of which are included in the SMAP Enhanced 245 L2 Radiometer Half-Orbit 9 km EASE-Grid Soil Moisture product, version 6 (O’Neill et al. 246 2023). Soil moisture and VOD are mapped at least every 3 days and at a 9 km spatial 247 resolution. For our analyses, we convert soil moisture and VOD to monthly anomalies 248 relative to the 2015-2025 long-term average for each pixel. To further assess the dry 249 conditions underlying the fire, we also use monthly precipitation estimates from the Met 250 Office’s HadUK-Grid[20] gridded and regional average climate observations dataset at 1 km 251 resolution, which we also converted to monthly anomalies (accessed 9/19/2025).

## 283 **Quantifying carbon emissions** 

284 For each burned grid cell, we apportioned carbon stocks based on the cover of different 285 vegetation types and mean carbon stocks of those vegetation types[12] . Aboveground 286 emissions were then calculated for each vegetation class within each burned pixel by 287 multiplying the burned fraction, the available biomass carbon, the combustion completeness 288 of different fuel types and the fraction of carbon (assumed to be 50%) in different fuel types.

290 For belowground emissions, we used two approaches to calculate our own bottom-up 291 estimates. The first used burn depths constrained by field data. Specifically, we used the 292 25[th] , 50[th] , and 75[th] quantiles of field-measured burn depths within burn severity classes (low, 293 moderate, high) in the ‘Bog’ as well as ‘Moorlands and Heathlands’ land cover classes. 294 Given that soil moisture measured via SMAP is at a very coarse scale relative to the finer295 scale mosaic of bogs interspersed throughout the landscape, the field sampling is an 296 approach that allows for microclimate or microtopography influences on burn depths. We did 297 not account for potential compression in bulk density, but we did only assume 50% of 298 combustion completeness. The mean bulk density of 58.2 ± 38.75 Mg m[[-3 ]] used in these 299 belowground emission calculations was determined from 20 measurements in a nearby 300 intact heathland.
