

### Baker_2025_Environ._Res._Lett._20_034028

## Summary

The research paper by Sarah J. Baker et al. investigates the impact of peatland fires on carbon emissions in the UK, particularly during dry years. The study highlights that peatland fires can significantly increase carbon emissions, contributing up to 90% of the UK's annual fire-driven carbon emissions. The authors used a fire emission model combined with high-resolution land-surface data and fire-weather indices to estimate that between 2001 and 2021, approximately 0.8 TgC was emitted from peatland fires. The study underscores the importance of protecting peatlands as a cost-effective strategy to mitigate climate change by preventing future emissions.

The research further explores the potential effects of climate change on peatland fire emissions, projecting that a 2°C global warming scenario could increase fire-driven carbon emissions by over 60% due to increased burn depths. The findings suggest that climate change-induced drier conditions could exacerbate the frequency and severity of peatland fires, leading to higher carbon emissions. This study serves as a critical indicator for other temperate peatlands globally, emphasizing the need for effective fire management and peatland conservation strategies to achieve climate goals.

### Journal of Ecology - 2023 - Coetsee - Soil organic carbon is buffered by grass inputs regardless of woody cover or fire

## Summary

This research investigates the impact of fire frequency and woody plant encroachment on soil organic carbon (SOC) in the savannas of Kruger National Park, South Africa. The study utilizes a long-term fire experiment, spanning approximately 70 years, to assess how different fire regimes affect SOC sequestration. The researchers analyzed SOC and carbon isotope ratios at various soil depths under different fire treatments, including annual high-intensity burns, triennial burns, and fire exclusion. The study found that while increased woody cover due to fire suppression led to higher SOC, the contribution of C4 grasses to SOC remained significant across all fire regimes. This suggests that grasses play a crucial role in maintaining SOC levels, challenging the assumption that increased tree cover alone significantly enhances carbon sequestration in savanna soils.

The findings highlight the resilience of C4 grasses in contributing to SOC, even under varying fire regimes and increased woody cover. The study emphasizes the importance of grass inputs in maintaining SOC stocks, suggesting that fire management strategies should consider the role of grasses in carbon sequestration. This research is relevant for ecological management and climate change mitigation strategies, as it provides insights into the complex interactions between fire, vegetation, and soil carbon dynamics in savanna ecosystems. The results suggest that maintaining a balance between tree and grass cover, rather than solely focusing on increasing woody biomass, may be more effective for enhancing carbon storage in these environments.

## Summary

This research paper explores the potential of sustainable agricultural practices to simultaneously enhance crop productivity and soil carbon storage, addressing the dual challenges of climate change mitigation and food security. Through a comprehensive global meta-analysis of 2975 paired observations from 510 publications, the study evaluates the impacts of four key practices: reduced tillage, cover cropping, complex crop rotations, and crop residue retention on the yields of maize, wheat, rice, and soybeans, as well as on soil organic carbon (SOC) stocks. The findings reveal that while significant gains in both yield and SOC were observed in only 18.1% of cases, a majority (55.6%) showed significant improvements in at least one of these outcomes. The study highlights that environmental conditions, such as warmer temperatures and water-limited areas, play a crucial role in determining the success of these practices.

The research underscores that sustainable agricultural practices do not inherently create conflicts between productivity and carbon sequestration goals but rather present management trade-offs. These trade-offs arise from the need to optimize practice-crop combinations to maximize either yield or SOC gains, depending on specific environmental and agronomic conditions. For instance, residue retention consistently increased both SOC and yields, while reduced tillage primarily enhanced SOC. The study also notes that the effectiveness of these practices can be enhanced by combining them, suggesting that practice stacking can alleviate trade-offs and maximize benefits. However, uncertainties remain regarding subsoil carbon responses and the net climate benefits of these practices, indicating areas for future research to refine strategies for large-scale implementation.

### rstb.2024.0001

## Summary

The research paper by Little et al. (2025) addresses the urgent need for advancing wildfire science in the context of changing global fire regimes, driven by climate change and human activities. The authors present perspectives from wildfire scientists in the USA, a historically fire-prone country, and the UK, an emerging fire-prone country, to identify priority research directions. Through a series of workshops, they highlight three key areas: understanding and predicting fire occurrence, behavior, and impacts; increasing human and ecosystem resilience to fire; and understanding the atmospheric and climate impacts of fire. The paper emphasizes the importance of fire behavior as a central theme that connects these areas and suggests that addressing these research gaps requires a transdisciplinary approach that incorporates diverse knowledge bases and perspectives.

The paper underscores the increasing complexity of wildfire challenges, including the rise of extreme wildfire events and their significant impacts on ecosystems, human health, and the economy. It calls for updating fire behavior models and danger rating systems to account for new extremes and novel fire phenomena. The authors also stress the need for international collaboration to enhance predictive capabilities and develop effective fire management strategies. Additionally, the paper highlights the importance of integrating social sciences, arts, and humanities to improve risk communication, policy-making, and community resilience. By outlining these research priorities, the paper aims to guide future efforts in addressing the multifaceted challenges posed by changing wildfire regimes globally.

### s11104-025-08031-z

## Summary

The mini-review by Pellegrini et al. (2025) explores the complex interactions between fire, plant, and soil processes, emphasizing the need for a bottom-up framework to understand fire's impact on ecosystem biogeochemistry. Traditionally, fire's effects have been viewed through a top-down lens, focusing on changes in plant biomass and composition. However, this review argues for a more nuanced perspective that considers the intricate plant-soil interactions and microbial processes that influence soil carbon and nutrient dynamics post-fire. The authors highlight key processes such as soil carbon saturation, nutrient acquisition strategies, and physical soil changes that collectively shape the biogeochemical feedbacks in fire-affected ecosystems.

The review underscores the variability in fire's impact on soil processes across different ecosystems, pointing out that while shifts in plant biomass consistently affect soil properties, the role of plant-soil interactions is increasingly recognized as critical. The authors call for integrating these interactions into fire models to better predict changes in soil carbon storage and nutrient cycling. By examining the interplay between top-down and bottom-up processes, the review provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the long-term ecological consequences of fire, which is particularly relevant in the context of changing fire regimes due to climate change and land-use practices. This perspective is crucial for developing more effective fire management strategies and for predicting the future dynamics of carbon and nutrient cycles in fire-prone ecosystems.

### s41467-025-59272-6

## Summary

The research paper investigates the global risk of wildfires across different timber production systems, focusing on the vulnerability of timber plantations compared to natural production forests. The study combines global datasets on forest management and wildfire occurrences from 2015 to 2022, analyzing data from 17 countries that account for a significant portion of global timber production and wildfire-affected areas. The findings reveal that temperate plantations are twice as likely to experience stand-replacing wildfires compared to natural production forests under similar environmental and anthropogenic conditions. This increased risk is particularly pronounced in major timber-producing countries like China and Russia. In contrast, tropical plantations do not show a consistent pattern of increased fire risk, with national variations ranging from significantly lower to slightly higher risks compared to natural forests.

The study highlights the critical role of timber plantations in meeting the rising global demand for wood, which is expected to increase significantly by 2050. However, the increased susceptibility of plantations to wildfires poses a threat to global timber security, especially as climate change is expected to exacerbate fire frequency and severity. The authors suggest that improving fire management practices, such as landscape-level planning and increasing plantation diversity, is essential to mitigate these risks. The research underscores the need for strategic interventions to balance the trade-off between the rapid productivity of plantations and their heightened fire risk, ensuring sustainable timber production in the face of climate change.

### s41558-023-01800-7

## Summary

The study investigates the impact of altered fire regimes on soil organic carbon (SOC) storage in global drylands, particularly focusing on savannah and grassland ecosystems. By combining datasets from fire-manipulation experiments across 53 sites, the research identifies that drier ecosystems experience more significant SOC changes due to fire than humid ones. This is primarily attributed to the reduction in tree biomass inputs in these regions. The study highlights that many ecosystem models underestimate SOC changes in drylands, suggesting that ongoing declines in fire frequencies have likely created a substantial carbon sink in these soils, which has been previously underestimated.

The research further explores the environmental variables influencing SOC changes, identifying aridity, precipitation seasonality, mean annual temperature, and soil texture as key factors. The study uses a meta-analysis approach to demonstrate that fire-driven SOC losses are more pronounced in drier and cooler environments with coarser soils. Additionally, the study finds that changes in tree biomass inputs, as indicated by δ13C measurements, are a significant predictor of SOC changes, with frequent burning leading to a shift from tree-derived to grass-derived biomass inputs. The findings underscore the importance of integrating fire management into climate solutions, particularly in savannah–grassland ecosystems, to enhance SOC sequestration and address the carbon-biodiversity trade-offs associated with fire suppression.

### s41561-024-01384-7

## Summary

The research paper investigates the temperature sensitivity of soil organic carbon (SOC) decomposition, focusing on the distinction between mineral-associated (protected) and particulate (unprotected) carbon pools. The study highlights that particulate carbon exhibits a higher temperature sensitivity compared to mineral-associated carbon, with this sensitivity being more pronounced in cooler climates. This differentiation is crucial as it influences the overall temperature sensitivity of bulk soil carbon stocks, which are vital for understanding carbon cycle-climate feedbacks. The authors utilize a globally gridded dataset to analyze the distribution and temperature sensitivity of these carbon pools, revealing significant variability in model predictions from Earth system models (ESMs) regarding the proportion of protected carbon.

The findings underscore the importance of accurately representing the distribution and temperature sensitivity of soil carbon pools in global models to improve projections of carbon cycle-climate feedbacks. The study identifies discrepancies in current models, which often underestimate the proportion of protected carbon, leading to potential biases in predicting soil carbon dynamics under climate change scenarios. By providing benchmarks based on observational data, the research offers a pathway to refine model formulations and reduce uncertainties, emphasizing the need for models to report soil carbon pool distributions in future climate model intercomparison projects. This work is relevant for researchers and policymakers aiming to enhance the predictive capabilities of climate models concerning soil carbon dynamics and their implications for global carbon budgets.

### s41561-025-01735-y

## Summary

This study investigates the global-scale shifts in marine ecological stoichiometry over the past 50 years, focusing on the elemental ratios of carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and phosphorus (P) in marine ecosystems. The research compiles an extensive dataset of 56,031 planktonic and 388,515 seawater samples collected from 1971 to 2020, spanning depths from the surface to 1,000 meters. The findings reveal significant deviations from the traditional Redfield ratio, which posits a consistent C:N:P molar ratio of 106:16:1 in marine organic matter. Instead, the study shows that planktonic C:P and N:P ratios are consistently higher than the Redfield ratio, indicating a widespread deviation from canonical stoichiometry across different oceanic regions and depths.

The research highlights dynamic changes in marine stoichiometry over time, with planktonic C:N and N:P ratios rising in the late 20th century before declining, suggesting a progressive alleviation of phosphorus limitation likely driven by increased anthropogenic phosphorus inputs. The study also observes depth-related patterns, with decreasing oceanic C:N and C:P ratios and increasing N:P ratios with depth, attributed to differential remineralization and microbial nutrient cycling. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating spatial and temporal variability into biogeochemical models to improve projections of marine ecosystem responses to global environmental changes. The study's insights are crucial for understanding how marine ecosystems might adapt to ongoing anthropogenic pressures and climate change.

### v1_covered_6e57e2fa-8f17-4450-93e7-975b221490dd

## Summary

The research paper documents the first megafire in the United Kingdom, which occurred in June 2025 in the peatlands of Scotland. This unprecedented event, known as the Dava Moor Fire, burned approximately 10,000 hectares, making it the largest wildfire in UK history. The study highlights the significant carbon emissions resulting from the fire, with peat combustion contributing to 85% of the total emissions, amounting to 39,338 MgC. The findings underscore the vulnerability of peatland ecosystems to drier and warmer climates, which increase the likelihood of severe fires and the associated carbon emissions that are functionally irrecoverable on timescales relevant to climate change mitigation.

The paper emphasizes the implications of such fires for climate change and policy. The combustion of peat, which takes centuries to millennia to re-sequester carbon, poses a significant challenge to achieving net-zero carbon goals. The study suggests that the UK and other regions with temperate and northern peatlands may experience more frequent and severe fires due to climate change, necessitating a reevaluation of fire management and peatland restoration strategies. The research calls for prioritizing the prevention of wildfires in peatlands and considering projections of soil moisture changes in restoration efforts to mitigate the risk of peat burning during fires.
