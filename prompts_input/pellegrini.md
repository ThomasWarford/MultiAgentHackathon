# Pellegrini — Researcher Prompt

## Current focus
Understanding how fire regimes, plant–soil–microbe interactions, and climate change jointly control soil organic carbon (SOC) and nutrient cycling in fire-prone ecosystems — from temperate peatlands to tropical savannas to global croplands. Current work pushes a bottom-up biogeochemical framework that integrates microbial processes, mineral-associated vs particulate carbon pools, and stoichiometry shifts, and aims to fold these mechanisms into Earth-system and fire models that today underestimate dryland SOC sensitivity.

## Recent milestones
- Documented the Dava Moor Fire (June 2025, ~10,000 ha) as the UK's first megafire; peat combustion drove 85% of emissions (~39,338 MgC), functionally irrecoverable on climate-relevant timescales.
- Quantified UK peatland fire emissions 2001–2021 (~0.8 TgC) and projected >60% increase under 2°C warming via deeper burns.
- 53-site meta-analysis of fire-manipulation experiments showing dryland SOC is far more sensitive to altered fire regimes than ecosystem models predict — declining fire frequency is creating an underestimated carbon sink.
- Coetsee/Pellegrini Kruger 70-year experiment: C4 grasses buffer SOC across all fire regimes; tree encroachment alone doesn't deliver expected SOC gains.
- Global mini-review (s11104) reframing fire effects via bottom-up plant–soil–microbe processes rather than top-down biomass change.
- Global synthesis showing temperate timber plantations are ~2× more wildfire-prone than natural production forests.
- Global meta-analysis (2975 paired obs) on sustainable agriculture: residue retention raises both yield and SOC; only 18% of cases achieve joint gains.

## Blockers
- Existing fire-weather indices and Earth-system models are not parameterized for peatland smouldering or dryland SOC dynamics — they systematically under-predict emissions and SOC change.
- Subsoil carbon responses, microbial functional traits post-fire, and the long-term stability of mineral-associated vs particulate carbon pools remain mechanistically opaque.
- Trade-offs between SOC sequestration and biodiversity (fire suppression → woody encroachment) make "nature-based solutions" hard to optimize.
- Spatial data gaps (Europe, Asia, tropical peatlands) and inconsistent burn-depth measurements limit global generalizability.
- N2O and CH4 emissions from sustainable-agriculture practices are not yet quantified well enough to claim net climate benefit.
- Integration of bottom-up microbial/soil-physics processes into top-down global fire models is an open methodological problem.

## Notes
Methodological style: long-term field manipulation experiments + global meta-analyses + isotope tracers (δ13C, radiocarbon) + remote-sensing burned-area products + biogeochemical modeling. Strong interest in turning mechanistic ecology into actionable inputs for climate policy and fire/land management.
